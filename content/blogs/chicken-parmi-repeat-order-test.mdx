A repeat visit can tell you whether a chicken parmi is worth keeping on your shortlist. Here’s what I look for before backing it again.

I’m based in Queensland, and eating parmis around Australia has given me plenty of reasons to try somewhere new. But returning to a place answers a different question. A good first plate earns my interest. Another good plate gives me a stronger reason to recommend it. Neither guarantees what’s coming out of the kitchen next week, but I’d rather build a favourite on evidence than keep dining out on one happy memory.

## Give yourself something specific to come back for

Before I decide a parmi deserves another order, I want to know what actually worked. “Loved it” is a perfectly good reaction over lunch, but it’s a pretty flimsy reference point a few months later.

Was the chicken juicy right through? Did the topping have enough sauce without drowning the crumb? Were the chips worth finishing? Did the salad taste like something someone wanted me to eat?

I don’t need a spreadsheet. A sentence in my phone is enough: “Good chicken, properly melted cheese, chips a bit soft.” That gives the next plate something concrete to live up to.

I also separate the meal from the afternoon. Good company can make a middling lunch feel terrific. My parmi ratings run from 0 to 10 and cover the whole plate, nothing off it. The memory I use for a return visit should follow the same rule.

## Return to a plate, not a photograph

When I reviewed the [Ramsgate Hotel at Henley Beach for the first time](/reviews/ramsgate-hotel), the parmi didn’t look particularly appealing at first glance. I lowered my expectations. Then I checked underneath the chicken schnitzel and found no soggy bits. That visit finished at 7.9/10.

That’s a useful reminder for a second order: remember what you ate, not merely what you saw.

A slightly different shape or a less tidy arrangement doesn’t tell me the cooking has slipped. Equally, a handsome blanket of cheese doesn’t prove the chicken underneath is any good. I want another enjoyable plate, not a replica for the camera.

If the first visit impressed you because the crumb held up underneath the toppings, check that again. If the chips were excellent, give them proper attention rather than treating them as background. Repeat the observations that mattered, not the photo.

## Keep the order comparable where you can

If I’m trying to find out whether a venue can repeat a good parmi, I’d keep my next order reasonably close to the first. Swapping the topping, changing the sides and adding extra sauce might produce a lovely lunch, but it answers a different question.

That doesn’t mean you owe a pub an experiment. Order what you fancy. Just be fair about what you can learn from it.

I’d also avoid inventing reasons for a difference. A dry piece of chicken is a dry piece of chicken. Without knowing what happened in the kitchen, I can’t pin it on a different cook, a busy service or a changed supplier.

For readers choosing where to eat, the useful information is the result: what arrived, what tasted good and what didn’t. The imagined explanation adds very little.

## Let the second visit change your mind

My [second Ramsgate Hotel parmi](/reviews/ramsgate-hotel-oct-2025) scored 8.2/10. I called the cheese perfect, and the plate earned a higher mark than my first visit.

I wouldn’t turn two meals into a promise of permanent consistency. What I can say is that I had two positively rated parmis there, with the later one scoring better. That’s a stronger basis for another order than one good meal on its own.

The same openness has to work backwards. If a favourite serves a disappointing plate, I shouldn’t mark it kindly because I enjoyed the previous one. Nor should one ordinary lunch magically erase everything that came before it.

I’d rather hold both facts together: it was good last time; this time it wasn’t. That’s more useful than declaring a place unbeatable or writing it off forever.

## Build a shortlist that can change

My advice is to keep a small, flexible shortlist rather than crown one permanent winner. Remember what you liked, roughly when you ate it and whether you’ve had it more than once. When recommending a venue, say whether you’re backing one visit or several.

If an otherwise enjoyable parmi had one weak point, a return order can settle whether that problem repeats. If most of the plate disappointed you, there’s no obligation to spend another lunch confirming it. I’d sooner try somewhere else.

A favourite should keep earning its place through the food. That leaves room for improvement, honest disappointment and the very pleasant discovery that your second order is better than your first.
