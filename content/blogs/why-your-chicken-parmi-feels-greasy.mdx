A greasy chicken parmi can have several culprits; here’s how I tell excess frying oil from cheese fat and ordinary moisture.

I want a parmi that tastes generous without leaving my mouth feeling coated halfway through. But a shiny surface isn’t automatically a fault, and a puddle underneath doesn’t always mean someone stuffed up the frying. Before I blame the kitchen, I want to know what I’m actually eating. A few well-chosen bites tell me more than a glance at the plate.

## A shiny top isn’t the same as a greasy bite

Melted cheese can release fat. Chicken releases juices, tomato sauce brings water, and dressing can wander across the plate. All of those can make a parmi look wet, but they don’t feel the same when I eat them.

For me, the useful distinction is between richness and a lingering greasy coating. Richness makes the next mouthful appealing. Excess grease hangs around after I’ve swallowed and starts blurring the chicken, tomato and cheese together.

I pay attention to whether I can still taste those separate ingredients. If every bite finishes with the same oily sensation, something is out of balance. If there are a few shiny beads on the cheese but the mouthful tastes clean and lively, I’m not docking it for looking like melted cheese.

## Check the cheese before blaming the fryer

The top is the easiest place to start. I take a small bite of topping with chicken, then compare it with an exposed edge that has little or no cheese. I’m not dismantling dinner; I’m checking whether the heavy feeling follows the topping.

When I reviewed [Embassy Bar & Kitchen](/reviews/EmbassyBarKitchen), I found a thick layer of mozzarella, a nice thick piece of chicken and not much sauce. The whole plate scored 4.6/10. Those notes don’t establish that the parmi was greasy, and I wouldn’t rewrite them to suggest it was.

They do show why “plenty of cheese” isn’t enough information to judge a topping. Thickness, moisture and the balance with sauce all matter. More mozzarella can mean a lovely, generous melt, or it can make the chicken harder to appreciate. I judge the result in my mouth rather than treating a heavy cheese layer as an automatic bonus or fault.

## Look underneath, but don’t pretend you watched it cook

Next I lift an edge and try a small piece of the underside. An oily crumb may leave a persistent fatty film on my lips. A crumb softened by sauce or chicken juices can be damp without that same coating. Sometimes both are happening.

A clear-looking puddle alone won’t settle it. Fat and watery juices can collect together, especially once I’ve cut into the chicken. I look at the food and taste it rather than trying to identify a liquid by colour.

There are several possible kitchen causes of excessive oiliness, including frying conditions and inadequate draining. What I can’t do from my seat is confidently announce the oil temperature or how long the chicken sat in a basket.

That distinction matters. “The underside tasted oily” is a useful observation. “The fryer was definitely too cold” is a guess dressed up as expertise. I’d rather be accurate than sound clever.

## Give the chips their own bite

Chips can make the whole meal feel heavier, but softness and greasiness aren’t interchangeable. A chip can be limp without tasting oily, just as a crisp chip can still leave too much fat behind.

When I reviewed [Dome Cafe, Kwinana](/reviews/dome-cafe-kwinana), the chicken had a crispy deep-fried coating, while the chips weren’t very crisp. The salad was fresh with juicy tomatoes, and the ham was a little cold. I scored the plate 5.5/10.

That’s a useful reminder to keep the observations separate. The chicken’s coating and the chips didn’t perform alike, even though both were fried. I wouldn’t turn “chips weren’t crisp” into a claim about greasy oil.

I also try a chip that hasn’t been sitting against the parmi. That gives me a better chance of judging the chip itself rather than whatever has run onto it.

## Ask for the change you actually want

If excess cheese is what makes a parmi feel heavy to you, asking for a lighter layer is more useful than asking for it “less greasy”. If the crumb itself tastes oil-soaked, extra tomato sauce won’t remove that oil. It may add brightness, but it doesn’t undo the problem.

My standard is simple: I want satisfying richness without a fatty coating taking over the meal. I rate the whole plate out of ten, and an oily mouthful matters because of how it eats, not because I’ve decided all visible fat is bad.

Where do you most often notice unwanted oiliness on a parmi: the cheese, the underside or the chips?
