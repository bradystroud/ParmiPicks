Hunger can flatter an average chicken parmi; here’s how I separate a welcome feed from a genuinely good plate.

I’m based in Queensland, and after eating and rating parmis around Australia, I’ve learnt that my appetite isn’t always a reliable witness. That first mouthful can feel magnificent simply because lunch was hours ago. There’s nothing wrong with enjoying the relief. But when I’m deciding whether a plate deserves a strong recommendation, I need something more useful than “I really needed dinner”.

## The review where I called myself out

When I reviewed [The Feathers Hotel in Burnside](/reviews/feathers-hotel-68), I gave the parmi **7.2/10**. I also admitted something worth keeping beside that number: I was starving after an hour’s wait and thought almost anything would have tasted good.

That doesn’t prove the score was wrong. It does mean my enthusiasm came with a qualification. I’d rather acknowledge that than pretend every review happens with exactly the same appetite.

The wait itself isn’t part of my rating. I judge the chicken, toppings, chips and salad, with nothing off the plate earning or losing points. But hunger follows me to the table whether I invite it or not. Recognising that influence helps me keep the verdict about the food.

For anyone choosing their next parmi, that distinction matters: being delighted that dinner has arrived isn’t quite the same as being delighted by how well dinner has been made.

## Give the first bite a narrower job

I reckon the opening bite should answer a small question: does this taste good right now? It doesn’t need to settle whether you’ve found your new favourite.

When I’m very hungry, hot chicken and melted cheese have a fairly easy job winning me over. I try to keep that initial pleasure separate from a final opinion. There’s still plenty of plate left to learn from.

A few mouthfuls in, I look for details I can actually name. Is the chicken pleasant to chew without relying on a mouthful of topping? Does the tomato sauce contribute flavour, or is it barely there? Are the chips good when eaten on their own?

This isn’t an argument for letting dinner sit while you conduct an inspection. Eat it while it’s ready. Just leave a little room between “thank goodness, food” and “everyone needs to order this”.

## A big feed can win the wrong argument

An empty stomach makes a generous piece of chicken especially persuasive. I appreciate a proper serving, but size answers “will this feed me?” more directly than “is this good?”

When I reviewed [Club Kawana in Buddina](/reviews/club-kawana), the big slab of chicken certainly didn’t go unnoticed. I also appreciated the unusual diced ham. Yet the plate finished at **5.5/10** because the poor salad and boring chips let down a great parmi.

That’s a useful example of why I rate the whole plate. An impressive centrepiece can dominate your attention, especially when you’re ravenous. It shouldn’t make the rest of dinner disappear from the verdict.

You can be properly full and still have eaten an average meal overall. Equally, enjoying the chicken doesn’t oblige you to defend everything beside it. Those opinions can comfortably share a plate.

## Describe dinner before declaring it brilliant

My simplest defence against hunger bias is to find a few concrete words before reaching for a big compliment.

“Loved it” tells a mate that I had a good time eating. “Juicy chicken, flavoursome sauce, disappointing chips” gives them something they can use. Specific observations also make it harder for sheer relief to write the whole review.

I don’t need every mouthful to reveal a fault, either. This isn’t about talking myself out of enjoying dinner. If the chicken is lovely and the sides are good, I can say so with confidence. The point is to know what earned the praise.

I’m also wary of the reverse mistake: treating a clean plate as proof of excellence. Sometimes I finish everything because I’m hungry. Eating it all records what I did, not necessarily how good it was.

## Keep the appetite, sharpen the verdict

I wouldn’t tell anyone to spoil their appetite before ordering a chicken parmigiana. Arriving hungry is part of the pleasure. I simply want my recommendation to remain useful to someone who isn’t feeling exactly what I felt that night.

Enjoy that first mouthful. Then notice whether the food keeps giving you reasons to enjoy it. A strong verdict should have a few edible details behind it, not just an empty stomach in front of it.
